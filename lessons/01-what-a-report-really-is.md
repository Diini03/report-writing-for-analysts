# Lesson 1: What a Report Really Is

## The one line to remember

> A dashboard shows you what happened. A report tells you what it means and what to do next.

That gap is the whole subject of this course. Most self taught analysts can build the first half. Almost nobody teaches the second half, and it's exactly the half interviewers test for.

## The scoreboard, and the analyst

Picture a football match. The scoreboard tells you the score, the shots on target, the possession. That's your dashboard, a set of facts with no opinion attached.

When the match ends, there's an analyst on TV. He doesn't read the score back to you. He tells you why the team won, what changed after halftime, what the coach needs to fix before next week. That's a report. Anyone can read a scoreboard. Getting hired as an analyst means being the person who can explain it.

## The three parts every report needs

| Part     | Question it answers                              | Missing it looks like                      |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Audience | Who is reading this, and what do they care about | Writing for yourself instead of the reader |
| Insight  | What did the data actually reveal, not just show | A chart with no explanation next to it     |
| Action   | What should the reader do now                    | A finding that goes nowhere                |

Leave any one of these out and you don't have a report. You have a pile of numbers with sentences wrapped around them.

## Example one, the Titanic

A chart can show first class passengers survived at a much higher rate than third class, close to 63 percent against 24 percent. That's the dashboard, a fact sitting on a page.

The report goes further:

> Survival wasn't really driven by class itself. Passengers in first class happened to be closer to the lifeboats. If designing a modern evacuation plan, priority should be based on physical position, not what ticket someone holds.

**Your turn.** Below is another true fact from the same dataset. Write one sentence of "report" on top of it, the way the example above did.

> Fact: female passengers survived at close to 74 percent, male passengers at close to 19 percent.

## Example two, COVID case data

The dashboard is a line chart, daily cases climbing in a region, updated every day.

The report says something a chart alone cannot:

> Case growth has outpaced hospital bed capacity for three straight weeks. Unless mobility restrictions return within five days, projected demand will exceed ICU capacity by the twentieth.

One is a picture. The other is a warning, with a deadline attached to it.

**Your turn.** Here's a raw fact. Turn it into a one sentence warning or recommendation, the same way.

> Fact: new user signups have dropped 30 percent over the last two weeks, right after a competitor launched a cheaper plan.

## Example three, a churn dashboard

The dashboard shows a KPI card, churn up four percent this quarter, broken out by region.

The report explains what's underneath it:

> The increase is concentrated among basic plan users who stopped watching two weeks before they left. That points to boredom, not price, as the real driver, so a re engagement campaign is likely to work better than a discount.

Same numbers as the dashboard. Completely different value, because now someone reading it knows what to do.

**Your turn.** Take this fact and write the "so what" sentence yourself.

> Fact: support ticket volume has doubled in the last month, and 70 percent of the new tickets mention the mobile app, not the website.

## Back to your own interview

When you were asked how you prepare a report after building a dashboard, the interviewer wasn't checking if you can describe a chart. They were checking if you can walk from a number to a decision. Every lesson after this one is built to give you that walk, step by step.
