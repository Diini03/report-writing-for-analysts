# Lesson 4: Writing With Clarity

## Structure alone is not enough

A report can follow the correct structure and still fail if the sentences inside it are vague, uncertain, or purely descriptive. This lesson addresses the sentence level habits that determine whether a well structured report reads as professional or as unfinished.

Four habits account for most of the difference between weak and strong analytical writing.

## State claims with a number attached

An unquantified claim is difficult to evaluate and easy to dismiss.

> Weak: Sales declined significantly in the East region.
>
> Strong: East region sales declined 18 percent, from 210,000 to 172,000.

If a claim cannot be supported with a number, the underlying analysis is generally not yet complete, and the claim should not be included until it is.

## Write with appropriate confidence

Uncertainty has a proper place in a report, in the limitations section, stated once and clearly. It does not belong scattered through every sentence in the form of qualifying language.

> Weak: It seems like delivery times might possibly be a contributing factor.
>
> Strong: Delivery times increased from 4.2 to 7.6 days over the same period, closely aligned with the timing of the sales decline.

The second version does not overstate certainty. It states what the data shows and allows the reader to weigh it accordingly, without undermining the finding before it has been read.

## Explain rather than describe

A chart already shows its own contents. Restating what is visible adds nothing.

> Weak: The chart shows that churn increased in the third quarter.
>
> Strong: Churn increased in the third quarter, concentrated among customers on the lowest priced plan who had reduced usage in the preceding two weeks, indicating disengagement rather than price sensitivity as the primary driver.

The first sentence describes a picture the reader can already see. The second sentence adds interpretation the reader could not have obtained from the chart alone, which is the actual contribution a report is meant to make.

## Make recommendations specific

A recommendation that defers the decision without specifying an action is not functionally different from making no recommendation at all.

> Weak: Further investigation into the shipping process is suggested.
>
> Strong: It is recommended that the operations team review the East region shipping contract before the next peak season.

The second version names a responsible party, a specific action, and a timeframe. It can be acted on directly. The first version cannot.

## Applying all four together

The difference these habits make becomes clearest at the paragraph level.

> Before: Looking at the data, it seems the West region performed reasonably well this quarter, while East and Central may have had some difficulties. This could be due to a number of factors and may be worth reviewing further.
>
> After: West region exceeded its target by eight percent this quarter. East and Central both missed target by more than ten percent, concentrated in the furniture category. The pattern is consistent with a fulfillment issue introduced by a new shipping vendor in July, rather than a decline in demand. Renegotiating the vendor contract before the next quarter is recommended.

Both paragraphs are built from the same underlying facts. The difference is entirely in precision, confidence, and whether the paragraph ends with something the reader can act on.
