# Lesson 3: The Structure of a Report

## A repeatable structure

Regardless of report type, professional analytical reports tend to follow the same underlying structure. Learning this structure removes the need to decide how to organize a report each time one is written, and it ensures nothing important is left out under time pressure.

The structure has six parts, always in the same order.

| Section        | Purpose                                        |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Title          | States the subject and time period covered     |
| Summary        | The complete message in two to four sentences  |
| Key findings   | The specific evidence, each point quantified   |
| Explanation    | The reasoning connecting the findings together |
| Recommendation | The action that follows from the analysis      |
| Limitations    | What the data does not fully support           |

## Title

A title should identify both the subject and the scope in a single line, for example, _Quarterly Sales Performance Review, Third Quarter_. A title without a stated scope leaves the reader uncertain what period the report is describing.

## Summary

The summary sits at the top of the report but is written last, once every other section has already been completed. This ordering matters because a summary is meant to condense finished analysis, not to predict what the analysis might find. A well written summary allows a reader with limited time to understand the entire message without reading further.

> West region exceeded its target by eight percent this quarter. East and Central both fell short by more than ten percent, concentrated in the furniture category. The shortfall is linked to a new shipping vendor introduced in July. Renegotiating the contract before the next quarter is recommended.

## Key findings

Each finding should be a specific, quantified observation. A finding without a number attached is closer to an opinion than to evidence.

> - Furniture category revenue declined 18 percent year over year in the East region.
> - Average delivery time increased from 4.2 to 7.6 days over the same period.
> - Complaints referencing late delivery increased by 340 percent.

## Explanation

This section is the one most frequently omitted by inexperienced analysts, who move directly from findings to recommendation. Its purpose is to state why the findings occurred and how they relate to one another, demonstrating that the analyst has interpreted the data rather than simply reported its surface.

> The timing of the decline aligns closely with the onboarding of a new shipping vendor in July. Underlying demand, measured by product views and cart additions, remained flat over the same period, indicating that the shortfall reflects a fulfillment problem rather than a decline in customer interest.

## Recommendation

A recommendation must state a specific action. General statements that defer the decision, such as suggesting further investigation without specifying what to investigate, do not satisfy this section.

> It is recommended that the operations team renegotiate the East region shipping contract before the next peak season begins.

## Limitations

Every analysis has boundaries, and stating them directly increases rather than decreases the credibility of a report. Omitting this section tends to invite the exact questions it would otherwise have preempted.

> This analysis covers the East and Central regions only, as the West region met its target. Delivery time figures were provided by the vendor and have not been independently verified.

## A complete example

Applying all six sections to a single, well known dataset illustrates how they work together.

**Title:** Titanic Survival Analysis, Implications for Evacuation Policy

**Summary.** Survival was more strongly associated with cabin location and gender than with the fare a passenger paid. Third class passengers were nearly three times more likely to die than first class passengers, an outcome primarily linked to distance from lifeboats rather than to ticket category itself. Evacuation protocols modeled on this pattern should prioritize physical proximity to exits over passenger classification.

**Key findings.** First class survival rate approximately 63 percent; third class approximately 24 percent. Female survival rate approximately 74 percent against a male survival rate of approximately 19 percent. Survival declined sharply for cabins located in lower and rear sections of the ship, independent of ticket class.

**Explanation.** Fare alone does not account for the survival gap. When findings are grouped by cabin location rather than by ticket class, class appears to function as a proxy for physical distance from lifeboats, indicating the underlying driver is structural rather than a direct effect of passenger category.

**Recommendation.** Evacuation protocols should be designed around real time physical proximity to exits rather than passenger classification.

**Limitations.** Cabin location data is unavailable for approximately 77 percent of passengers. The cabin based finding should therefore be treated as directional rather than conclusive.
